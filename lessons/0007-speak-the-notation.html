Lesson 7

Speak the notation

No new ideas today. You will rewrite everything you already know in the notation the field uses, so papers and videos stop looking like a foreign language.

Your goal. Learn six pieces of notation: kets as sums, two-qubit kets, complex conjugates, bras and the inner product ⟨ψ|φ⟩, the dagger †, and the tensor product ⊗. Each one starts from vectors and matrices you already use, then shows the notation, then asks you to use it. Earlier ideas come back as review along the way. Then watch two short assigned clips, listed at the end.

From memory

Plain vectors first. Try these without opening the Reference panel.

1. Kets are sums

You already read |0⟩ as the column (1, 0) and |1⟩ as (0, 1). Any one-qubit state (a, b) splits into those two pieces:

ab = a 10 + b 01 = a|0⟩ + b|1⟩

The field writes states this way, as a sum of kets. The number in front of each ket is its amplitude. For example, |+⟩ = (1/√2)|0⟩ + (1/√2)|1⟩, which is often shortened to (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2. |0⟩ and |1⟩ are called the standard basis.[1] |ψ⟩ (“psi”) and |φ⟩ (“phi”) are the usual names for an arbitrary state.

Given: |ψ⟩ = (3/5)|0⟩ − (4/5)|1⟩. Asked: write it as a column. Enter decimals.

2. Two-qubit kets

The same split works with four entries. Each position gets a ket named by its label, A's digit on the left:

(a, b, c, d) = a|00⟩ + b|01⟩ + c|10⟩ + d|11⟩

The order 00, 01, 10, 11 is dictionary order, with the left digit most significant. Your uniform state is ½(|00⟩ + |01⟩ + |10⟩ + |11⟩), and the 11-marked state is ½(|00⟩ + |01⟩ + |10⟩ − |11⟩). The ½ in front multiplies every term.

Gates act term by term. To apply a gate to a sum, apply it to each ket and add the results. CNOT is easiest in this form: it flips B when A is 1, so CNOT|10⟩ = |11⟩, CNOT|11⟩ = |10⟩, and CNOT leaves |00⟩ and |01⟩ alone. That is the same swap of entries 10 and 11 you used in Lesson 4.

Given: (1/√2)(|01⟩ − |10⟩). Asked: enter its four amplitudes in the order 00, 01, 10, 11.

3. Complex conjugate

The complex conjugate of z = a + bi flips the sign of the imaginary part: z* = a − bi. Physics writes the star z*. Math books often write a bar, z̄. Both mean the same thing.[2]

Why you need it: multiply a number by its own conjugate and you get its squared length, always a real number and never negative.

z* z = (a − bi)(a + bi) = a² + b² = |z|²

Check with i: i* = −i, and (−i)(i) = −i² = 1 = |i|². Real numbers are their own conjugates.

Given: z = 2 − 3i. Asked: the real and imaginary parts of z*, then z* z.

4. Bras and the inner product ⟨ψ|φ⟩

A bra ⟨ψ| is the ket |ψ⟩ turned into a row, with every entry conjugated. Turning a column into a row and conjugating is called the conjugate transpose.[1]

|ψ⟩ = ab   ⟹   ⟨ψ| = (a*   b*) = a*⟨0| + b*⟨1|

A row times a column is a single number. ⟨ψ|φ⟩ is the bra ⟨ψ| times the ket |φ⟩, with the two middle bars merged into one. This is the inner product, written ⟨ψ|φ⟩ from now on:

⟨ψ|φ⟩ = (a*   b*) cd = a*c + b*d

For real amplitudes the conjugates change nothing, so this is your Lesson 6 dot product. For complex amplitudes the conjugate is what keeps ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ = |a|² + |b|², the sum of probabilities. Without it, S|1⟩ = i|1⟩ = (0, i) would give i · i = −1 for a state whose probabilities add to 1.

The standard basis kets make ⟨0|ψ⟩ = a and ⟨1|ψ⟩ = b. So the bra ⟨k| picks out an amplitude, and the probability of outcome k is |⟨k|ψ⟩|². You will see that form of the measurement rule everywhere.

Given: |+i⟩ = S|+⟩ = (1/√2)|0⟩ + (i/√2)|1⟩, and |+⟩ = (1/√2)|0⟩ + (1/√2)|1⟩. Asked: ⟨+i|+i⟩, then the real and imaginary parts of ⟨+|+i⟩. Remember to conjugate the bra only.

5. The dagger † and “unitary”

The conjugate transpose works on matrices too, and is written with a dagger: M† means swap rows with columns, then conjugate every entry. The bra is just the dagger of a ket: ⟨ψ| = |ψ⟩†.

S = 100i   S† = 100−i   H† = H

H has only real entries and is symmetric across its diagonal, so H† = H.

Definition A matrix U is unitary when U†U = I, where I is the identity matrix.[1] In Lesson 5 you met the inverse U⁻¹. This definition says U⁻¹ = U†: to undo a gate, apply its dagger.

Given: S and S† above. Asked: multiply S†S and enter the four entries.

See how This is the one-line reason gates preserve inner products, which the Lesson 6 clip derived. The bra of U|φ⟩ is ⟨φ|U†, so ⟨Uφ|Uψ⟩ = ⟨φ|U†U|ψ⟩ = ⟨φ|I|ψ⟩ = ⟨φ|ψ⟩. You do not need to reproduce this, only to read it.

6. The tensor product ⊗

The Reference panel already shows |01⟩ = |0⟩ ⊗ |1⟩. Here is the general rule for two one-qubit states. Each entry of A's state multiplies all of B's state, in dictionary order:

ab ⊗ cd = acadbcbd ← 00← 01← 10← 11

In kets, |a⟩ ⊗ |b⟩ = |a⟩|b⟩ = |ab⟩. Papers often drop the ⊗ symbol and write two kets side by side.[3]

Gates combine the same way. Acting with U on A and V on B at the same time is the matrix U ⊗ V, and it acts piece by piece: (U ⊗ V)(|φ⟩ ⊗ |ψ⟩) = U|φ⟩ ⊗ V|ψ⟩. Doing nothing is I. So your gate names translate like this:

In this courseStandard notation
H on AH ⊗ I
H on BI ⊗ H
H on bothH ⊗ H

Given: |+⟩ = (1/√2, 1/√2) and |−⟩ = (1/√2, −1/√2). Asked: enter the four amplitudes of |+⟩ ⊗ |−⟩.

Global phase, in notation

Multiplying a whole state by a number c of length 1, such as −1, i or any eiθ, is a global phase. It changes no probability, because |⟨k|cψ⟩|² = |c|² |⟨k|ψ⟩|² = |⟨k|ψ⟩|². So |ψ⟩ and c|ψ⟩ count as the same state.[4]

A relative phase is different. It multiplies only some terms. |+⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2 and |−⟩ = (|0⟩ − |1⟩)/√2 differ by a sign on one term, and H tells them apart perfectly.

Your circuit, rewritten

A circuit reads left to right, but the matrix product reads right to left. The first gate sits next to the state, because it acts first. So “H on A, then CNOT, then H on A” applied to |ψ⟩ is:

(H ⊗ I) · CNOT · (H ⊗ I) |ψ⟩
