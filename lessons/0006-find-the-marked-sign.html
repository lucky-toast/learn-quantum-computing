Lesson 6

Find the marked sign

A friend hides a minus sign on one of four amplitudes. Design one circuit that reads it out with certainty.

Your goal. You solved this in chat. Rebuild it here from memory, then explain with dot products why it can work at all. Then watch one short assigned chapter, listed at the end.

From memory

Try these without opening the Reference panel.

A hidden mark

Start from the uniform state (½, ½, ½, ½). A friend flips the sign of exactly one amplitude. If they mark 11, you get (½, ½, ½, −½). There are four possible marked states, one per position.

Postulate Measuring gives each outcome with probability equal to its amplitude squared.[1]

The obvious first try

Given: the 11-marked state (½, ½, ½, −½). Asked: apply H on A, then H on B, and enter the four amplitudes. Use paper, one pair at a time. Enter decimals, such as 0.5 or −0.5.

Design the readout

Given: all four marked states, listed below. Asked: build one circuit. Run on every input applies it to all four and shows what you would measure. You can use up to 8 gates, which is more than any solution needs.

00-marked (−½, ½, ½, ½) · 01-marked (½, −½, ½, ½) · 10-marked (½, ½, −½, ½) · 11-marked (½, ½, ½, −½)

What an exhaustive search says

I checked every circuit built from these seven gates, ignoring overall sign:

  • No circuit with fewer than 3 gates tells all four apart.
  • Exactly two 3-gate circuits do: H on A, CNOT, H on A and H on B, CNOT, H on A.
  • Reading out the marked position itself needs 5 gates. No 4-gate circuit does it.

Why it can work

The dot product of two lists of numbers multiplies matching entries and adds the results. For example, (1, 2, 3) · (4, 5, 6) = 4 + 10 + 18 = 32. A dot product of 0 means the two states are orthogonal. This rule is for real amplitudes. Complex amplitudes need one more step, which comes later.[2]

Given: |0⟩ = (1, 0) and |+⟩ = (1/√2, 1/√2), plus the 00-marked and 11-marked states above. Asked: compute both dot products. Enter decimals.

Math fact Unitary matrices preserve dot products: (Uv) · (Uw) = v · w.[2] Gates are unitary, so no circuit can change how much two states overlap.

Your circuit sends the four marked states to |00⟩, |01⟩, |10⟩ and |11⟩, which are orthogonal. That is only possible because the marked states were already orthogonal. The circuit does not create the difference. It turns a difference measurement cannot see into one it can.

Inner products, from the source

“Quantum Circuits”, Lesson 3 of Understanding Quantum Information and Computation, by John Watrous (Qiskit on YouTube).

Which part
Open the video's chapter list and watch the chapter called Inner products. You don't need the rest of the video yet.
Why
Watrous explains today's dot product in standard notation. He calls it an inner product, and his version also handles complex amplitudes, which you will need when S and other phase gates return.
Listen for
Where the complex conjugate enters the definition, and how he defines orthogonal and orthonormal.
